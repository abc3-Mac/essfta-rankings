The President’s Message
Submitted by Cristy Joy
It is with a saddened heart, I write of the ESSFTA loss of a dedicated Springer member and Board
of Governor. Lori DiLorenzo passed on the morning of March 25th, 2024. The ESSFTA will be
celebrating her life and accomplishments. Please share with all of us your wonderful stories
throughout her membership with our organization.
I reflect on the unwavering passion and dedication we collectively share for the well-being and cel-
ebration of our English Springer Spaniels. As the proud president of the ESSFTA, I extend a
heartfelt thank you to all our valued renewed members and our new ESSFTA members as we roll
into the Spring of 2024. The support of the organization stands as a testament to a time when we
come together not just as members, but as a family united by an unbreakable bond forged through
our love for the English Springer Spaniel breed.
As we look forward to the coming year, our aspirations for the club are both ambitious and excit-
ing. Our primary goal is to deepen our engagement with the ESSFTA community, fostering an
environment where both dogs and their owners can thrive in harmony. We aim to continue to ex-
pand our educational outreach with our working committees that cover a range of topics. Look for
the reports at the May 2024 ESSFTA Annual Meeting – May 3rd and 4th in Denver Colorado.
Furthermore, we're committed to continue to have our local annual events, inclusive and enjoyable
for all members. By implementing these initiatives, we not only enrich our club's offerings but also
reinforce our commitment to promoting the well-being and happiness of dogs in our community.
Together, let's make this year a remarkable chapter in our club's history, marked by growth,
learning, and an unbreakable bond between us and our Springers.
As we work on the bond with our Springers, keep your confidence high!!!
Tackle you event training with a calm, positive outlook! A nice, simplified approach you can adapt
to in your personal life may include "Three P's" technique: Patience, Praise, and Persistence”
▪ Patience: Training is not a race. Allow both you and your dog the time to understand and
adapt to each lesson. Deep breaths, steady progress.
▪ Praise: Our breed thrives on positive reinforcement. Offer enthusiastic praise for a job well
done. This builds a happy, confident learner.
▪ Persistence: Consistency is key. Stick with your training efforts, even when progress seems
slow. Small, regular steps lead to big achievements.
Good Luck in the ring, on the course, in the barn and in the field!!!
Have a great Spring!! As the nights are getting lighter I feel more excited to start seeing you en-
joying being outdoors enjoying your Springer :-)
Best regards and GO SPRINGERS!!!!! Cristy

G reat Advice and Terrible Suggestions
Watching, asking and learning
Let’s learn to listen to advice as closely as our dogs listen to us.
How many times have you seen a dog in the ring: working, leaping, shining eyes saying to the judge:
“Watch me. I am special. “? How often does a dog catch your eye and you see something that makes
you want to ask that exhibitor for their secrets? It never hurts to ask.
Five gracious, wise women recently confided advice they have received and advice they would give
to help every Springer fancier enjoy pursuits with our dogs.
Susan Schneider, Peridot
Best advice: “ ‘Another day, another dog show.’ Told to me by the great handler and beloved all-
rounder judge, Anne Rogers Clark. She was the first woman to go Best in Show at Westminster KC. I
took this to mean each day is an opportunity to learn and do something great.
‘Great things are accomplished only by the perfection of minor details’ is the motto of my former boss
and master dog man George Alston. I took this to mean the difference between good and great is hard
work and dedication.

Kim Bolster
Best advice: “When you are standing at the first obstacle (or the start of the obedience exercises),
look at your dog and say to yourself, ‘I really LOVE this dog! What a great dog you are!‘ Then, smile at
your dog. It brings home the joy of your dog and the partnership you share. To me, this helps keep it
all in perspective. At performance events, you can often find exhibitors/owners who show their frustra-
tion (usually from their mistake) who take it out on the dog. Ignoring the dog, pulling it into a crate --
crushing acts that are so hard to watch. A good motto to remember is: ‘Dogs are with us for such a
short time -- make it count.’ That quote reminds me that agility should be fun. An NQ is not the end of
the world!
If asked by any dog owner starting agility, I would tell them, ‘Treasure your time with your dog: prac-
tice and competition.’ I would convert them to smiling often at their dog. I would also tell them to be
prepared to laugh: your dog will surprise you and keep you very humble.”
Worst advice: “Being offered ‘cookie cutter’ advice: the same advice for every dog to handle every
dog’s specific problem. I did not follow this sort of advice because I knew my dog. All advice must be
filtered through your knowledge of your specific dogs' personality.”

T he Bench Governors are meeting regularly to continue to advance key initiatives for our members. We

J udges Education
Our ESSFTA Judges Education seminar held at the AKC Advanced Judges Institute in Orlando, Florida
in December was a HUGE success. We had 24 attendees, one of the largest contingencies of judges/
candidates in attendance amongst all breeds. Dr. Erin Kerfoot, Judges Education chair, presented an
updated Power Point and other educational materials. Janet Warner volunteered time out of her packed
schedule to assist Erin. Several dogs were used in the hand-on portion. The evaluations by the partici-
pants were OUTSTANDING with Erin and her team receiving an overall average rating of 4.93 out of
5.0.
In addition to many positive comments about the presentation in general, several noted that having a
live dog at the presentation to demonstrate key points was extremely helpful and that the variety of dogs
for the hands-on clearly showed breed type while showing diversity of acceptable styles. In terms of are-
as for improvement, some noted that having an Illustrated Standard would be a huge help – and we’re
work ing on that!
The Judge’s Ed Committee is meeting on a regular basis to refine their approach. The next scheduled
Judges’ Ed presentations will be at the September 2024 National Specialty (Purina Farms) and Spaniel
Club (Knoxville, TN) in January. The Chattahoochee club has also contacted Erin to help put together a
“Springer Enthusiast” focused educational program. If clubs are interested in conducting a similar educa-

AKC Updates
AKC has changed the rules around the 3 point major for RWD and RWB majors at Specialties. If the
number of dogs/bitches competing is double the number needed for a 5 point major, a 3 point major will
be awarded to RWD/RWB unless the club applies to exclude it.
Unless a Breed Club, in its Application to the AKC to hold a specialty show, chooses to exclude, the fol-

I NBREEDING VS LINEBREEDING VS OUTCROSSING
THE DEFINITIONS:
INBREEDING:
Inbreeding is the very close breeding of relatives. Parent-offspring and sibling-sibling breedings are what
most people are referring to when they mention inbreeding, but technically pairings such as aunt/uncle-
niece/nephew, cousins, and grandparent-offspring are also inbreeding. The percentage of inbreeding in-
creases depending on how closely related the animals are. For instance, if a female is bred back to a
male that is her sire but also her cousin, that’s an even higher percentage of inbreeding than a simple
sire-daughter pairing of otherwise unrelated animals.
LINEBREEDING:
Linebreeding is technically a form of inbreeding. However, linebreeding deals with pairs that are
related more distantly than first-generation relatives. For example, it might involve breeding two
animals that have a common grandparent. The object of linebreeding is to bring an animal in the
pedigree forward again and increase its genetic influence over the resulting offspring. This is re-
ally the goal of all forms of linebreeding and inbreeding.
OUTCROSSING:
An outcross is when two completely unrelated animals who share no common ancestors within four to six
generations are bred.

ESSFTA Health and Genetics
Submitted by Viginia H. Huxley PhD
Who gives a fig about having a CHIC number?
Apparently, there has been some discussion on social media about the ESSFTA parent club requiring a
CHIC number for the top awards. Some feel it is AKC withdrawing more $$ from our already depleted
wallets; some say it is just a useless hurdle that is tiresome and of no value; some say they simply don’t
care. Hmm. Seems to me we need to take a few steps back, provides some background and provide
some answers. At the end, should there be more questions, concerns, objections, let’s keep the discus-
sion going. First, for those not in the “know” what the heck is a CHIC number? Back up one more step
– the Canine Information Health Certification (CHIC) program was formed between breed parent
clubs and the Orthopedic Foundation for Animals (OFA) as a data base of health information that pro-
vides a resource for multiple audiences. For example, as breeders, the data base provides us with health
information about the dogs we wish to use in our breeding program. For veterinary and basic science
researchers, the data is of assistance in the conduct of research on current conditions and recognition
of em erging areas of concern.
For owners, the data base provides information about the breeds that may impact their longevity and
quality of life. The criteria of what is needed to achieve CHIC certification for any breed is not deter-
mined by the AKC, or the OFA but by the parent club for the breed. Therefore, if you go to the OFA
website to look up the criteria, you will find that they vary by breed: https://ofa.org/chic-programs/
browse-by-breed/
For our English Springer spaniels (https://ofa.org/chic-programs/browse-by-breed/?breed=EN )
there are a list of required and optional health screening areas. There are 5 elements that need to be
fulfilled to obtain a CHIC number. Four of these elements address conditions known to be distributed
widely among field, bench and companion springers and the fifth element addresses the future of the
breed.
(1.) Hip dysplasia using an OFA evaluation of a hip X-ray or PennHip evaluation at 24 months
or older. Hip dysplasia has proven to be a condition that can result in significant limitation in
mobility and pain. Presently, while the genetics behind the development has proven elusive, the
readings have aided in reducing the prevalence of severe dysplasia in our breed. Of note, the
Health and Genetics committee is using the CHIC data base to look at the incidence of dysplasia
occurring in one hip only when the general “belief” is that dysplasia always occurs in both joints.
THE SPRINGER SPOTLIGHT • 25 • March 2024

(Cont)
Submitted by Viginia H. Huxley PhD
(2.) Elbow Dysplasia by OFA radiographic evaluation. Elbow dysplasia has only come into the
spotlight recently in ESS as a concern as springers have become more involved in performance
sports. The front end of the dog takes the brunt of the force of landing after a jump and the
structure of the elbow is very vulnerable to injury given the lack of muscle, tendons and ligaments
around the joint (as compared with hips). Further, as those with dogs with elbow dysplasia will at-
test, breakdown, pain and difficulty moving occurs early in life and is difficult to treat. Again, the inher-
itance of elbow dysplasia is not known.
(3.) ACVO Eye Exam. There are a variety of eye disorders known to effect sight in the Springer
that presently only be detected during an exam by a board -certified veterinary ophthalmologist.
(4.) Progressive Retinal Atrophy (PRA) DNA Test. Presently there is one form of PRA in the
ESS for which a DNA test exists. Results from a certified laboratory are registered with OFA.
(5.) DNA extracted from a blood sample previously banked with the ESSFTA/University of
Missouri or presently participation in the CHIC/DNA Repository.
The next 5 areas are OPTIONAL.
The first two, tests for fucoidosis and phosphofructokinase (PFK) deficiency represent progress by
the fancy in the near elimination of 2 metabolic disorders in the breed that proved to be fatal in dogs
while working. These 2 diseases are not yet “gone” but are present in very low numbers as carriers ra-
ther than affected dogs.
Testing for degenerative myopathy (DM) is available and optional because during initial evaluation of
the DNA test it was found that an appreciable number of ESS were carriers of the gene. Like PRA symp-
toms of dysfunction in dogs with 2 bad copies of the gene is not always evident. Basically, DM exists in
ESS and can be propagated by breeding. Informed breeding with knowledge should keep the prevalence of
the disease from increasing among ESS. Consider the recommendation for testing for DM as cautionary.
Autoimmune thyroiditis occurs with some frequency in the breed resulting in conditions ranging from
difficulty in breeding, failure to thrive, and susceptibility to disease. While the condition can be easily
treated it can occur at any point in a dog’s life; it is recommended that testing occur at different times
during the lifetime of the dog.

Finally, there are published reports of ESS suffering from heart problems albeit our health survey did
n ot highlight cardiovascular concerns.
In any case, the CHIC data base will accept results from a basic cardiovascular exam. As alluded to ear-
lier, one of the main reasons for collecting these data is to facilitate evaluation of the health of the breed.
A dog does not have to “pass” all the evaluations to be given a CHIC number, the results just have to be
available. OFA collecting these data greatly helps the breed providing us an early warning system for
health concerns arising or abating. As breeders we really should not balk at being forth coming. No
organism is a ‘perfect specimen – one day’s genetic features maybe tomorrow’s cure or disease – it all
depends. As breeders and fanciers we must accept that data are just that, data. By that I mean there are
no simple answers (like “Never breed to a carrier or affected dog”) as we are working with complex
beings and long -term strategies for maintaining and strengthening the breed. ESS do not presently have
“lethal mutations: as can occur when breeding a merle to a merle. Closing our eyes and dismissing the
sharing of information is irresponsible and fosters rumor mongering and diminishes trust. Some suggest
the process is expensive. Really? Think of how much we spend in one single weekend of shows –
whether conformation, agility, or scentwork! How much do we invest in stud fees, bringing a healthy
litter into this world, training and maintaining health? Personally, I applaud the parent club for only
requiring 5 elements – the most expensive being the radiographs.
Finally, I would argue that we should applaud performing the basic evaluations on all our ESS, not just
those participating in breeding programs or being high-fliers in the conformation ring. Why? Well, first,
there is much to be learned from the littermates of the breeding studs and bitches as to the traits within
a pedigree. Second, especially for the dogs in the performance world, the health information impacts
directly on which activities will influence the quality of their lives. A dog with elbow dysplasia might
breakdown quickly if steered towards agility, field or even tracking, for example but live full happy
fulfilling morbidity-free lives in scentwork.
I would happily entertain comments and questions.
