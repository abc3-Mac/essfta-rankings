greatly appreciate your hard work.

bers for the benefit of the ESSFTA.

PRESIDENT BIO
Cristy Joy, President, ESSFTA
Cristy lives on an acreage north of Lincoln Nebraska with her Husband Mike and their 12 Eng-
lish Springer Spaniels. She was raised in a small community in Southeast Nebraska. She is a
country girl at heart, enjoys people, loves to hunt with her dogs and believes in being active in
her organizations. Cristy cherishes her family. They share in her love of Spaniels with their own
dogs and participation in the Springer community.
Cristy is a member of the English Springer Spaniel Club of Eastern Nebraska and the Platte
River English Springer Spaniel Club. Cristy has had the privilege to serve in many roles of our
organization. She was the ESSFTA Corresponding Secretary and currently serves as a Board
of Governor At-Large. Cristy is very active at the local and national level including the following
roles: Local Club President for several years; chaired and served on Field Trial committees;
marshalled, fund raised and many other roles at Field Trials and Hunt Tests. Cristy is as a qual-
ified Hunt Test judge and a certified Field Trial Gunner.
Cristy bought her first ESS in 2004 for hunting and fun with the family. She attended the 2011
National in Beatrice Nebraska and was all in. She joined her husband Mike’s ESS club and
started working with the group. Cristy has had great mentorship with her Club mates. They have
provided a strong base to her field trialing preparation. She was fortunate and had the opportuni-
ty to purchase Ripper as a started field trial dog. Ripper was a dog that was great teammate to
learn the game. Ripper had 27 AKC field trial points and a Master Hunter title. She also has
strong mentoring and friendships with Professional Handlers across the country. Cristy’s family
is also involved with the English Springer Spaniel breed. She is very fortunate to spend time with
her family as she works with her dogs. It has paid off and she has championed and titled dogs
while competing in local field trials, local Hunt tests and c ompeted in numerous Open and Ama-
teur Nationals in the US and Canada. Her Dog CHIEF received 2nd place at the 2021 National
Amateur Championship in New York with Cristy handling.
Cristy is a registered Architect with a career dedicated to her passion in public service and ethi-
cal conduct. Cristy will apply the skills she has acquired in her 34-year business career. This in-
cludes her training to lead an organization while working with people and their concerns. Cristy
is a team player creating a culture of openness, interaction and community. These skills will be a
great asset working with the board as she serves the Parent Club.

Shaving your dog’s coat – should you or shouldn’t you?
By: Jennifer Oldfield Marketing Manager Albert North Veterinary Clinic
.
Structure of the coat on a double coated dog (Image created by Brook Wilkins)
There are several different types of textures on dog’s fur, but in a general breakdown there are two coat types; single coated
and double coated. A single coat means that there is only a top (or over) coat that grows all over the body with no different
undercoat. Breeds such as Shih Tzus, Poodles, Bichons, for example, are ones with a single coat. These breeds can be shav-
en generally with the only thing potentially occurring to the coat is over time it may become softer or it may have a slight
colour change. Even with only a single coat you want to be careful on when you shave them, especially if you shave them
right down, as this, although may appear to feel cooler, leaves the dog exposed to the possibility of sunburn. A dog with a
coat shaved right down in the height of summer should not spend any length of time in direct sunlight. With the simple fact
that there is only one coat the hair grows back normally and even after a shaving.
A double coat means there is both a top (or over) coat made of tougher guard hairs and a bottom or (under) coat that is
thick and soft. Breeds such as Pomeranians, Shetland Sheepdogs, Siberian Huskies, Samoyeds, are examples of double coated
dogs. With a double coated dog they need to be groomed by brushing throughout the year but most heavily done in the
spring when a major shedding period occurs. As the weather warms up the thick undercoat starts to do a complete shed, it
detaches from the body and is often described as molting. When you look at a dog in shed, they have “tufts” of fur that is
soft and dense peaking through the longer guard hairs of the topcoat, this is called molting.

Shaving your dog’s coat–should you or shouldn’t you? (Cont.)
by: Jennifer Oldfield
All this dense undercoat needs to be brushed out of the dog or Stage 3 from the top image above occurs. The coat be-
comes impacted and matted, preventing air from being able to move between the dog’s topcoat and their skin. Once all this
undercoat is removed the air can circulate between and through the hairs of the topcoat keeping the dog cool, while the
topcoat keeps the skin protected from the sun. This topcoat can also protect the dog’s skin from fly and mosquito bites.
The question still remains then, why not shave them and just keep them out of the sun? For one, they may not actually be
cool even if they are out of the sun, the topcoat can help to keep the heat off the skin itself and unlike people, dogs do not
sweat through their skin. Dogs sweat by panting and in all but northern breeds, through the pads of the feet. Shaving them
actually removes some of their natural ability to stay cool. Another reason is that when the hair does begin to grow back it
tends to do strange things. For some, it may mean having patches that don’t grow at all, or that don’t grow both types of
coat layers (top and under), older dogs often have issues with proper regrowth and then for others (which happens most
often) the undercoat grows in faster than the topcoat (since the topcoat isn’t meant to shed extensively it grows extremely
slowly) so now that protective topcoat is matted into the undercoat. Dogs like this generally appear as though they have
thyroid issues. The hair looks fuzzy and varies in length all over the body. This doesn’t mean the coat will forever stay this
way. Most of the time with regular brushing and the next shed cycle the topcoat will get longer while the undercoat sheds
away, eventually leaving the coat the way it once was with long topcoat guard hairs and a thick shorter undercoat. One oth-
er thing to note about those topcoat guard hairs – they actually prevent the dog from getting wet. Due to the coarseness of
the guard hairs water rolls off of this topcoat keeping the undercoat dry, which in the winter is important to keeping the dog
warm and dry.
All of this being said there may be times where it is necessary to shave a double coated dog. In surgical/medical situations
the coat must be shaved or if the undercoat has become so matted it cannot be combed out, shaving is the only solu-
tion. Once the coat begins to grow in, keeping them brushed and free of matting will prevent the need to shave them in the
future.
Bottom line?
The ideal situation: Keep the hair brushed, remove all the undercoat and allow the dog to remain with their natural ability to
keep themselves cool and protected from the sun and some bug bites in the summer and warm and dry in the winter.

ENGLISH SPRINGER SPANIEL FOUNDATION
Randy Capsel
Communications and Outreach Report:
Recently a discussion arose involving the various orthopedic and genetic disease testing results that were
available for posting on the Orthopedic Foundation for Animals and it was found that many individuals
were not aware that multiple health and genetic disease test results could be posted on the site. While
there is the Canine Health Information Center (CHIC) program that is partnered with individual parent
clubs, there is also availability for breeders and owners to provide other genetic test results for recording
within the OFA database.
A good starting point for determining how specific genetic test results a breeder or owner may possess
can be provided to the OFA is to access this particular application form page: https://www.ofa.org/
application-forms
There exists a specific form which allows for the breeder or owner possessing genetic testing results to
submit those results to the OFA for inclusion with their specific dog. While there is a fee, it provides a
wonderful opportunity to have a central location where those test results can be compiled for each specific
animal. There is a disclaimer that the submitter allows permission for the OFA to verify with a testing
laboratory the results submitted, and that the information submitted can be released as per the usual OFA
posting processes.
As the ability to conduct various genetic testing of our animals expands it is important to utilize such
database systems that will promote the health and well-being of our animals. It is hoped that as
knowledge of these resources is made known that breeders and owners will use the resources to provide
an open and transparent communication avenue for those interested in future breedings and those
researching dogs for their own homes. I would encourage anyone interested in this option to explore the
OFA website more deeply and be proactive in providing more in-depth information.
And along the same lines of multiple options, I want to remind everyone of the multiple donation options
available through the Foundation donation site. We understand not everyone is comfortable submitting
donations online. If you have a donation planned but prefer to submit by mail, there is a downloadable
form available at the following link: https://englishspringerfoundation.org/giving/donation-form
There you will find a link to the Donation Form that is a Word format document. Contact on the donation
form is being updated at this time, but we encourage anyone that needs the option of submitting a
donation by mail to use the form. Within the form you will find multiple donation options. There are the
various levels of “Springer Friends” designated by the giving amount. There are also options of Memorial
Gardens with text only and text and photo. Additionally, there are multiple specific purposed gift categories
which include the following: Dave Hopkins Memorial Fund, Archives Fund, Epilepsy Fund, General Fund,
Science Fund, Eye Disease Fund, and Cancer Fund. Each and every donation is very much appreciated
and always put to good use to further the health and wellbeing of the English Springer Spaniel.
During the Foundation June 2022 meeting the Officers and Board will be provided an update by Dr.
Miyadera and her continued PRA research project. Look for future updates coming soon.
A reminder that the Breeder’s Showcase will again be offered at the 2022 National Specialty and is
sponsored by the Foundation.
If there are any specific objectives or information you want me to focus upon please let me know and I will
do my best to update accordingly.

Blastomycosis
Submitted by Billie Kerfoot
In 1999, I was showing my springer Scarlett at agility trials. We had competed in trials on the Eastern Shore in
Virginia in September. In November I noticed pustules along Scarlett’s groin area. I took her to my vet, who
treated her for hot spots. However, the pustules soon became oozing lesions and I pointed out that these did
not look like normal hotspots. This vet did not seem alarmed and simply increased her pred nisone. When, by
the end of December, these lesions did not improve with regular treatment for hot spots, I took her to another
vet who had just moved to the area from Virginia Beach. Because he had practiced in an area w here fungal infec-
tions were a little more common, he realized these lesions could be more than hot spots and ordered a biopsy.
It took about two weeks for the university lab to get the results back – a rare fungal infection called Blastomyco-
sis, which is an extremely hard infection to cure. We started Scarlett on treatment immediatel y. After six weeks
on the meds, however, the lesions on her skin opened up, causing Scarlett excruciating pain. My new vet was
able to stick a scope into the wound to see her abdomen, where the spores had burst ope n! There was no
choice but to put her down, two months before her 8th birthday, and seven months after contracting the dis-
ease.
Although Blastomycosis is not a common fungal infection in most areas, with all the traveling we do with our
dogs, it is good to have information about the disease. Blastomycosis is usually caused by the in halation of spores
into the alveoli of the lungs. A pulmonary disease develops and the yeast forms, spreading through the lymphatic
system and blood vessels to the eyes, skin, bones, and other organs, causing local lesions which look almost like
poison oak blisters. Blasto can be contagious to other dogs if their immune system is comp romised, or they
breathe in the spores. And because open skin lesions could possibly have some spores in them, it is a good idea
to wash your hands thoroughly or use rubber gloves if you suspect this fungal infection.
Although this disease can be contracted by both humans and dogs, cats for some reason, do not seem to be as
susceptible. What does seem to be the major factor is a weakened or compromised immune system. Dogs with
a good immune system may only get a mild cough, and then it goes away without ever having to be treated. In
the United States, the disease is most prevalent in the warm, moist environment found along the Ohio, Missouri,
Tennessee, St. Lawrence, and Mississippi River valleys. It is very common in the Southeastern United States. Blas-
tomycosis has also been diagnosed in the Middle Atlantic States and southern Great Lakes. If left untreated, the
disease can be fatal to both dogs and humans.
The incubation period ranges from three weeks to three months. Fever, depression, loss of appetite, and weight
loss are common clinical signs. Lameness, testicular inflammation, seizures, coughing, enlarged lymph nodes, and
several of other signs may also occur with Blastomycosis.
Although the Blastomycosis fungus may spread throughout the entire body, it seems to target certain body sys-
tems, especially this skin, the lungs, and eyes of dogs.
The skin form is the easiest to diagnose. Dogs with the skin form often have chronic draining skin
lesions, especially around the paws. However, the lesions can occur anywhere (my dog had them in her groin
area). The lesions have little raised bumps like poison oak which rupture and become oozing ulcerations which
begin to crust over. Onset is gradual and can often be misdiagnosed, especially in areas where veterinarians may
not be familiar with the disease. These lesions do not respond to antibiotics, so if a lesion appears which does
not respond to normal procedures, do not delay! Demand a cytology and have it sent it to a pathologist and get
your answer in three to five days.

Blastomycosis (Cont.)
Submitted by Billie Kerfoot
In cases affecting the lungs, some degree of respiratory distress is usually present such as cough ing
and wheezing. Symptoms can be very minor and often go undetected until too late. When x rays are taken,
Blasto can even look like cancer! Because this fungus spreads through spores, in an x ray it appears as little white
globby patches everywhere. This is very similar to an x ray of a dog with cancer which has sp read to the lungs,
which is why Blasto is sometimes misdiagnosed as metastatic cancer.
For the eye form, many veterinary ophthalmologists are well versed in dealing with it. Dogs with the eye form
have red, painful, and swollen eyes which may cause sudden blindness. If you have a dog with an eye problem that
hasn’t resolved after seeing your family veterinarian, ask them about a referral to an eye special ist.
Diagnostic procedures include fungal culture, fungal serology, radiography, and hem atology. Cytol-
ogy, biopsies, and/or histopathology are required to diagnose Blastomycosis conclusively. Cytology may be per-
formed in the veterinarian's office using fluid that is draining from an open wound or is aspirated from a nodule
or lymph node. Since the organism is usually shed in large numbers in the draining lesions, Blas tomycosis may be
diagnosed in the office with cytology. Biopsies may also be obtained from affected areas. Biopsies should be
cultured for up to six weeks as this is a slow growing fungus. However, if Blasto is suspected, you
should consider starting medication as soon as possible. Histopathology is the microscopic examination
of cells within a tissue sample which is obtained and sent to a veterinary pathologist for this diagnostic test. This
process may take a few days for the results.
There is a new antigen test (enzyme immunoassay or EIA) for Blastomycosis, that may enable
veterinarians to diagnose this condition more quickly and accurately. Ask your veterinarian about the availability
of the new antigen urine and serum tests if your dog is suspected of having Blastomycosis.
The medicine of choice is antifungal drugs like itraconazole, ketoconazole, or fluconazole. But like much with this
disease, it’s not that easy. First, antifungals can cost a lot; depending on the size of the patient, the drug alone can
run into the thousands. It doesn’t clear up quickly, either. Dogs who are being treated can take the medication
for months on end. And sometimes the medication themselves can have nasty side effects (all medications have
the potential for side effects, but antifungals are more likely to make patients feel sick than most drugs we use).
Medicine should be taken a minimum of three months with monthly tests to check the immune system and kid-
ney function. In many cases, however, it takes several months for this disease to be eradicated. If the disease be-
comes systemic, the medicine of choice is Amphotericin B. However, this drug is very toxic and can cause vomit-
ing, diarrhea, and kidney damage. Therefore, the dog must be monitored very closely. If the fungus does become
systemic, it is quite often fatal.
If you live in an area where Blasto can be contracted, one way to be preventative is to make sure your dog is
getting a good immune system booster. Bottom line is that the more informed we as dog owners are, the more
we can help our vets diagnose our pets.
