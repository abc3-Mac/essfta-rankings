Field Trial Experience
Submitted by: Cathy Flanders
F ield Trial Experience
O n September 22, 2023, I attended my first ever
ESSFTA Field Trial competition. The trials were
held in beautiful Upstate New York (Canisteo, NY)
a nd run by the Southern Tier Spaniel Field Trial As-
sociation. I was fortunate to get an insight into how
field trials are run by ESSFTA President, Cristy Joy,
in attendance from Nebraska as well as club mem-
bers and the Marshall, Kim Cubitt from Ontario
Canada. I was in AWE watching these English
Cristy Joy & Bella
Springer Spaniels do what they were originally bred
to do, locating, and flushing the pheasant, retrieving
it, and returning it to their handler. On Sunday I re-
turned to take pictures. Rain does not deter an ESS
from doing their job.
I encourage all of our members to go to a trial, walk
with the gallery, volunteer as a “shagger” and watch
the outdoor beauty of our sport. I hope you will be
as addicted as I have become as a member who has
participated on the bench/performance side.
Thanks to the Southern Tier Club, Sunrise Kennels
(Gary Wilson and Cindy Goode-Wilson) and congratulations to the winners.

Membership Report As Of October 11, 2023
Provided by Membership Secretary, Cassie Salmons
Hello there! My name is Cassandra (Cassie) Sammons, and I’m the new ESSFTAvmembership secretary. I’m recently retired and have
been on the BOD of New England English Springer Spaniel Rescue for over 30 years. I currently co-own Wallylama’s Just a Classic
(Frodo) with Clemencia Saavedra and am learning about the conformation world. I also have a second ESS, Calli, who just turned 1,
who will probably be meeting you at the dock diving events (she won’t stay on the boat!)
I am a CPA/MBA/MIS/MSA, IT Auditor and Privacy/Computer Security Consultant. As you can guess, I love to learn! I look forward to
meeting many of you at Nationals. Now for the membership report:
We currently have 834 members in the ESSFTA. 44 new have joined this year.
There are several new things about this year’s renewal process you need to know.
1. The dues have increased for the 2024 year from $35 to $50 (individual) and $50 to $75 (family), and $10 (no
increase) for juniors. These increases will help us pay for the new website, Breed Illustrated Standard updates, Judges Educa-
tion, pamphlets and mailings associated with the latter two items as well as member communication. The discussion on dues
was held at the 2022 Annual meeting. If you have questions, please contact your Governor.
2 . You will receive an invoice early in January for your dues by email. It will have a link to a form to update your personal
information and a link to PayPal. (just like Nationals registration). The membership renewal form will include contact
information and the member's preferences of activities (these are important so we can separate mailings)
3. We need you to complete the entire Renewal Form to ensure that we have the latest information (Name, Spouse
Name (if family membership), address, phone, email etc.) on you to input into our new website. If you do not have an email on
file, I will contact you and mail your invoice if necessary. If you do not get an invoice via email after I announce it on the
ESSFTA members group, or by January 15th please email me at c.sammons@comcast.net and I will send you the invoice.
4. If you have a family membership, please remember that each member is entitled to a vote. Each member must
provide a separate e-mail and related information for ESSFTA to send out the ballot to both parties. Please ensure you include
both emails on the Renewal Form.
5. The deadline for paying your dues is February 1. The bylaws state that “No member may vote whose dues are not paid
for the current year.” I plan to send out several reminders.
6. Members who join after September 1 each year have the option of waiting till the next year to make their member-
ship active.
7. Please remember for Conformation/Performance awards you must be a member for 6 months prior to the end of
the awards year which is January 1-December 31. (July 1 deadline for renewal),
8. Please remember for Field awards you must be a member for 3 months prior to the end of the awards year which is
January 1-December 31. (September 1 deadline for renewal),

PRA RESEARCH:
The English Springer Foundation’s largest grant of $100,000 has funded, through the Canine Health Foundation,
the first phase of PRA research by Dr. Keiko Miyadera, DVM, PhD, Penn Vet. She has shared her Phase 1 findings
with the Foundation’s board, and will be sharing those findings at a presentation at the National Specialty Show on
October 10. She will also provide an outline of her Phase 2 research, again funded by the Foundation. We are so
appreciative of the support, participation, and donations made by so many “Springer People” from all interests –
conformation, performance, hunting, and field trialing. At the National, Dr. Miyadera will be collecting samples and
conducting eye exams, with particular interest in:
Affected dogs that she has previously examined;
Any dog with vision issues;
Any dog related to an affected dog in the study.
Dogs that have not yet been tested and examined who may provide valuable information,
especially if it is possible that they are genetically affected or related to genetically affected
dogs in the study.
Please Note: Our PRA Phase 2 study is now focused on the categories listed above. Known
genetically normal and carrier dogs will not be prioritized for eye exams at the National.
Eye exams are for Dr. Miyadera’s research; they are not designed to fulfill routine eye
exams/OFA requirements.
RARE BLEEDING DISORDER DISCOVERY:
Dr. Steven Friedenberg, DVM, PhD Dip. ACVECC, University of Minnesota and Dr. Molly Racette, University of
Wisconsin have discovered a rare bleeding disorder in English Springer Spaniels; they will present their findings at
the National. They have developed a technique to provide genetic testing for the disease and will collect samples
(using cheek swabs) at the National in order to gather more information; let’s support this important work.
The Foundation will fund the collection of cheek swab samples at the National for the first
100 Springers that participate. After we reach 100 samples, the Foundation will offset the
cost of testing at the National Specialty by 50%, providing $25 of the $50 cost. After the
National, the cost of testing will increase to $65.
Your participation is encouraged and appreciated!

registrations in the order received. Your Springer’s pedigree would be helpful but isn’t
required. Here’s the link: https://z.umn.edu/esspfib

DAMS:
A 2022 study has identified a genetic mutation in English Springer Spaniels called DAMS (DYSERYTHROPOIETIC
ANEMIA AND MYOPATHY SYNDROME). A family of Swedish show-type Springers revealed a number of affected
dogs that died from the disorder, which was also identified in the 1990’s in two distantly related dogs from
Australia. DAMS is early onset and ultimately fatal; affected Swedish dogs were euthanized between the ages of 1-
7 years. The symptoms are anemia and impaired muscle function and muscle wasting. The condition is currently
considered to be quite rare.
A statement from the English Springer Club (UK) Health Coordinators, Louise Scott and Lesley Field, regarding
DAMS is now on the English Springer Foundation’s web site, www.englishspringerfoundation.org Many thanks to
our UK friends for the work they have done to prepare and circulate this statement.

Making contact with agility wisdom
Zealots. Addicts. Aficionados. Once bitten by the agility bug, Springers come alive jumping, tunneling, and
weaving. Dogs and trainers in most parts of the country have suffered through a seemingly unending, scorching
Summer. Fall is here and we are ready to fly, weave and bring home the ribbons. Obsession to locate and enter
the next trial overtakes humans.
The AKC introduced agility competition in 1994 in Houston, Texas. Springer trainers had mastered their moves
and were ready to compete.
Alan Strauss, an AKC tracking judge, veteran obedience competitor and dedicated agility trainer believes
“Enthusiasm and teamwork are the basis for a competitive agility training program. My dogs having fun with me is
my first training goal.” Strauss starts training “as soon as possible. Puppies are great to teach because they are
little learning sponges. Agility requires basic obedience skills such as ‘come when called’, ‘sit’, and ‘stay’. You can
teach these things at any age.” Agility competition comes later. Strauss begins dogs between two and three years
of age; explaining, ”It all depends on the dog. Some are quick learners, while others take a bit longer. Building
drive in an agility dog is extremely important. Make every experience fun. Play with your dog, act stupid, run
around and have them chase you. Keep that tail wagging and keep that fire in their eyes.”
Which obstacles are the dogs’ favorites? Strauss says, “Each dog is different, but all my dogs love the tunnel;
sometimes a little too much.” Some dogs hear the siren call of the ‘tunnel suckers’. ”Weave poles and contacts”
require more training than running and jumping obstacles. Mastering “reliable weave poles in completion has been
the hardest. I have disqualified countless times because of (weaves). I worked with good instructors to help us.
Going back to the fundamentals in training helped each dog work through the problem. Making it fun and building
drive helped the most.”
Experience with several Springers has taught Strauss many lessons. He cautions, “Don’t try to progress too
quickly. Break down each obstacle skill into small increments. Slowly build the skill until you have the complete

“ Moving fast, praise, and treats!” Humans can’t resist the “teamwork, camaraderie of friends, and doing something
with dogs,” explains Kim Bolster, obedience trainer, longtime rescue advocate, fundraiser, and ESS foster mom for
MAESSR (maessr.org).
Bolster’s methods for introducing her dogs to agility have ” varied over the years, having trained five Springers in
agility. The majority were rescues so they came to me with varying amounts of training acquired at different stages
of their lives. My current agility dog came to me as a puppy. I chose to compete with her first in Rally while training
agility foundation skills. She did not start actively competing in agility until she was 3 years old.”
Being prepared to enter a trail “varies greatly by dog and personality. You get better with each dog you train. You
learn which foundation skills will benefit a young dog. I probably pushed my first dogs into competition too early. I
wanted to be sure with my current competitor that she was comfortable and ‘ready’ for the trial atmosphere and
courses.”
Which obstacles does Bolster think dogs like best? “Most dogs I have watched at class and at trails, love tunnels.”
Most difficult? “ Weave poles.
The handler worries about weaves, which transfers to the dog. Since the entrances to the weaves are always differ-
ent, this presents more difficulty to many dogs.”
http://www.clickerdogs.com/2x2_weave_training.php
Bolster strives to work carefully to prevent dog injuries, advising, “ Start low and work up; jump heights, A-Frame,
teeter, etc. Don’t push height until dog bones are fully formed and your dog has confidence on each particular piece
of equipment.”
Every dog has a different temperament and athletic condition. Bolster has seen trainers “push to train too fast. A
friend pushed her dog on teeter training. He was faced with a situation on the equipment that he was not prepared to
experience. It took over a year to re-train the teeter before the dog would go on any teeter anywhere.”
Are there any habits Bolster’s first agility dogs learned that she wishes she could have trained to prevent? “ Yes.
Excitement barking to go into the ring escalated as he got older and became barking on course. Today, I would re-
direct that excitement to tugging or some kind of play. At that time, I just kept jackpoting treats which encouraged
him more.”

Windle Ewing began agility training
1999. Ewing believes dogs love to play and that from a dog’s viewpoint, agility is a chance to play with the humans
they love. He enjoys agility’s “mental stimulation”. Why? Because ”each course is unique and challenging.”
When Ewing begins agility training varies. He explains,
“ I have started dogs as young as 9 months and as old as three years. Age is not a barrier as to when to start a dog
in agility.
“ Similarly, each dog’s preparation to compete in trials must be adapted to the personality and skills of the dog.
“Length of time (necessary to be ready to compete) is based on the dog's aptitude, attitude, owner/handler's experi-
ence level, and type of obstacle performances trained.”
Which obstacles does Ewing think dogs enjoy most? “Whatever is rewarded the most.” A core principles of teach-
ing any dog ( or any other learner) anything is the principle, ‘that which gets rewarded, gets repeated’ is core princi-
ple of positive, reward-based training.
Ewing has a suggestion for why many dogs learn proficient weaving more slowly than master of other obstacles,
suggesting, “Weaves tend to take longer to attain consistency. Weaving is not a ‘natural’ behavior like jumping, run-
ning, and climbing.”
Injuries, the dread of every dog trainer, require careful consideration. Ewing says, “We have been fortunate, hav-
ing only minor muscular and bruising injuries. Years ago, we experienced some nasty tire crashes, but the lowering
of the tire height and/or exclusion of the tire has greatly reduced the risk for my dogs. Currently, I would say the sport
is at its highest level of safety. Improvements in equipment, training techniques, and venue oversight are all attributa-
ble. “
Ballwork, treadmill, acupuncture, massage, chiropractic, cold laser, stretching, and swimming are a few of the sup-
ports useful for keeping athletic dogs in better condition.
